The tunnel

In 1945 the army started digging into a hillside outside Nasukarasuyama to build tanks underground, away from the bombing. The war ended before any machinery arrived and the tunnel was left as it was: three shafts, five cross-passages, about 600 metres in total, three and a half metres high.

Shimazaki, who had been brewing in the town since 1849, moved sake into it. The rock holds at around ten degrees all year without anything being done to it, which is close to ideal for ageing sake and considerably cheaper than a cold room.

You can walk it for ¥200. They hand you a tablet at the entrance and you go in on your own, past thousands of bottles stacked against the walls, most of which belong to other people.

That's the other half of it. For a few thousand yen you can put a bottle in the tunnel with your name on it and come back for it in five, ten or twenty years. People do this for children being born, and there are bottles in there now with collection dates in the 2040s.

If you want one reason to come back to Tochigi in twenty years, that's it.

What ageing does

Most sake is drunk within a year and is built for that. Leave it somewhere cool for a decade and it changes direction entirely: the fruit goes, the colour deepens towards amber, and what comes up instead is closer to nuts, dried fruit, soy and caramel. Some people find it wonderful and some people find it undrinkable, and there's not much middle ground.

Shimazaki bottle the aged stuff under a separate name, Jukurokko, so you can tell it apart from their everyday Azuma Rikishi. Start with the young end and work back.

What to try

Azuma Rikishi junmai
The house sake, unaged, and what the town actually drinks. Dry, straightforward, good warmed. Try this first so you know what the tunnel is doing to the rest.
Around ¥1,400 · 720ml
Jukurokko yamahai junmai
Yamahai to begin with, so it has acid and body before it ever goes underground. A few years in the rock rounds it off. Warm it to about 45° and it opens right up.
Around ¥2,500 · 720ml
Jukurokko daiginjo koshu
The odd one. A daiginjo is built to be fragrant and drunk young, so ageing one is a contradiction — and it works. Amber, dense, and nothing like what the label suggests. Drink it cold in a small glass.
Around ¥5,000 · 720ml
Whatever the oldest open bottle is
Ask. There is usually something with fifteen or twenty years on it that isn't on the list, and this is the only place you will be offered it.
Verdict

Two hundred yen to walk through a piece of the war that's now full of sake, and the only cellar in Tochigi that will hold a bottle for you until 2046. Go on a weekend and give it two hours.
